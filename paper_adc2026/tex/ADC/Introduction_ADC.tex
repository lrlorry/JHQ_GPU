\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Compressed approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search must balance query
quality, memory and execution cost. JHQ~\cite{jhq} exposes this balance through
a hierarchy: after inverted-file (IVF) routing, a compact primary code scores
$n_c$ candidates at cost $O(n_cM)$, and a per-dimension residual code refines
only $c_k$ survivors at cost $O(c_kd)$. The original method leaves the refinement
factor $\alpha$ in $c_k=\alpha k$ externally specified. This raises two
questions: \emph{how should the primary representation execute on a GPU}, and
\emph{how many survivors should be refined}?

For execution, we exploit the Cartesian construction of JHQ's primary
codebook. Additivity gives an exact decomposition of its byte-indexed distance
table into smaller tables, which we combine with coalesced candidate access and
packed loading. The identity is the mathematical basis of the design, rather
than a new quantisation algorithm. Grouped small tables are established in
ADC~\cite{fastscan,quickeradc,ivfrabitq}; the question here is which grouping
works for JHQ's representation and execution path. Our comparisons vary table
granularity and access layout while holding the represented distance fixed.
They show why table size alone is an insufficient design criterion.

For refinement, a small fixed budget can discard useful candidates, while a
large one may repeat work without changing the returned neighbours. We calibrate
$\alpha$ by comparing sampled top-$k$ sets with those produced at a generous
reference budget. This label-free signal is available to the index itself, but
its usefulness depends on sampling risk and amortisation. We test both against
fixed alternatives and an offline ground-truth reference.

Our two contributions are an execution design for JHQ's Cartesian primary
representation, with measured conditions under which factorisation and packed
access help, and an output-stability policy for its residual budget, with a
held-out evaluation of selection quality and calibration cost. Evaluation
against CPU JHQ and modern GPU baselines establishes their operating range on
one device; it does not imply uniform superiority across recall targets or
workloads.
