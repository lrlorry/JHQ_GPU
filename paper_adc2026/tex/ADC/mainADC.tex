\title{JHQ-GPU: Representation-Driven GPU Execution and
       Workload-Adaptive Hierarchical Refinement}
\titlerunning{JHQ-GPU}
\author{Rui Luo \and Mengxuan Zhang\thanks{Corresponding author.}}
\authorrunning{R. Luo and M. Zhang}
\institute{Australian National University, Canberra, Australia\\
\email{Rui.Luo1@anu.edu.au}, \email{mengxuan.zhang@anu.edu.au}}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Hierarchical quantisation scores candidates with a compact primary code and
refines a small survivor set. We study two GPU decisions for JHQ: how to
execute its primary representation and how much refinement a workload needs.
Its Cartesian codebook permits exact decomposition of each 256-entry distance
table into two 16-entry tables. We combine this factorisation with
candidate-parallel scanning and packed access. Controlled comparisons show
that smaller tables do not always produce faster scans. We select the residual
budget using sampled top-$k$ agreement with a generous reference budget,
without ground-truth labels or a learned predictor. A held-out experiment on all six
datasets compares fixed budgets, sampled and full-pool calibration,
enumeration and an offline oracle. With 128 calibration queries the selected
arm gains $1.05$--$2.77\times$ over fixed $\alpha=100$ on ten of twelve
cells, repaying calibration in an estimated $1$--$36$ subsequent 500-query
batches; on the remaining dataset the sufficient budget lies at or beyond the
tested grid, where the rule is slower than the constant it replaces. Six-dataset system
comparisons establish operating regions alongside IVF-PQ and CAGRA. The results
link JHQ's execution choices to its representation and quantify the quality
and cost trade-offs of refinement calibration.
\keywords{Approximate nearest neighbour search \and GPU \and Quantisation \and
Adaptive query processing}
\end{abstract}
