\title{JHQ-GPU: Representation-Driven GPU Execution and
       Workload-Adaptive Hierarchical Refinement}
\titlerunning{JHQ-GPU}
\author{Rui Luo \and Mengxuan Zhang\thanks{Corresponding author.}}
\authorrunning{R. Luo and M. Zhang}
\institute{Australian National University, Canberra, Australia\\
\email{Rui.Luo1@anu.edu.au}, \email{mengxuan.zhang@anu.edu.au}}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Hierarchical quantisation scores many candidates with a compact primary code
and refines only a small survivor set. On a GPU, its efficiency depends both on
how the primary representation executes and on how much residual refinement a
workload needs. We study these decisions for JHQ. Its Cartesian primary
codebook permits an exact decomposition of each 256-entry distance table into
two 16-entry tables. We combine this representation with candidate-parallel
scanning and packed access. Controlled comparisons show that the smallest table
does not give the fastest scan: additional lookups can outweigh the benefit of
reducing table storage. We then select the residual budget using the system's
own top-$k$ output stability on sampled queries, without ground-truth labels or
a learned predictor. A held-out comparison with fixed budgets, full-pool
calibration, exhaustive enumeration and an offline oracle measures both its
benefit and its sampling risk. With 128 calibration queries, the selected-arm
throughput gain is about $1.10$--$1.47\times$ over the fixed reference, with
estimated repayment in $4$--$19$ subsequent batches; some samples still exceed
the chosen recall-loss threshold. Experiments on six datasets show distinct
operating regions for JHQ-GPU, IVF-PQ and CAGRA, rather than a
universal winner. The results support adapting execution to JHQ's representation
and choosing refinement jointly with calibration cost and quality risk.
\keywords{Approximate nearest neighbour search \and GPU \and Quantisation \and
Adaptive query processing}
\end{abstract}
