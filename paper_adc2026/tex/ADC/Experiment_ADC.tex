\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experi}
\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
Measurements use one RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 170 SMs), a Xeon Platinum 8470Q
host (208 logical CPUs), CUDA 13.0, driver 595.71.05 and cuVS 26.08.01.
Table~\ref{tab:data} lists database sizes. OpenAI, BGE and Stella conversion
removes non-finite vectors, normalises nonzero vectors and holds queries out
with seed 42; Stella queries are corpus samples. All systems use the same
exported vectors and squared-L2 top-10 truth, without further normalisation.

Timing covers host query input to host result output, including transfers
and synchronisation. Frontiers process all $n_q$ queries in one call using
1,024-capacity buffers. JHQ reports batch size divided by mean time over three
warmed calls; cuVS reports mean QPS over three runs. Frontier QPS excludes
one-time calibration, accounted for separately through break-even analysis.
Vogue cold-build recalls of 0.9852, 0.9842 and 0.9849 establish an observed
variation of roughly $10^{-3}$; smaller changes require repeated evidence for
interpretation. Single-index differences retain their measured values.

JHQ uses $M=d/8$, $B=B_r=8$, 39 training points per centroid, eight coarse
iterations and 100,000 residual-training vectors. All JHQ frontier points use
this training setup and probes $\{8,32,128,256,512,1024\}$. Frontier
calibration uses $S=32$, $\epsilon=1$ and $\alpha_{\max}=100$ on the query
pool itself. The separate held-out evaluation uses $S=128$ and
$\alpha_{\max}=200$ (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}); its quality results do not
validate the frontier's calibration protocol.

IVF-PQ sweeps 8-bit subcodes with 96--768-byte codes on the four smaller sets
and 128--512-byte codes on the larger sets. CAGRA sweeps fp32/int8, graph
degree 32/64, search width 1/2 and intermediate top-$k$ 32--2,048 where
feasible. These configurations offer different memory and quality trade-offs;
GPU memory is not matched.

Curves retain nondominated clean points. Matched-recall ratios linearly
interpolate $\log(\mathrm{QPS})$ within measured coverage, without
extrapolation; they are descriptive comparisons, not significance tests or
necessarily runnable configurations. The implementation and plotting scripts
are available in the project repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/lrlorry/JHQ_GPU}}

\subsection{RQ1: end-to-end operating regions}
\label{sec:rq1}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} compares JHQ with IVF-PQ and CAGRA fp32.
CAGRA-int8 leads JHQ in 13 of 15 comparable target cells, including
$1.6$--$2.9\times$ gains in the ten cells below recall 0.95.

\label{fig:frontier}

\paragraph{Operating regions and stage costs.}
At matched recall, CAGRA fp32 is level or up to $1.5\times$ faster on the
768-dimensional datasets; JHQ is $1.3$--$1.8\times$ faster on the OpenAI
datasets. These cross-dataset observations do not isolate a dimension effect.
Separate JHQ stage measurements show refinement taking 57\% of timed work on
Vogue and 70\% on OpenAI-3072 at $\alpha=100$, $\mathit{nprobe}=32$.
The refinement share motivates budget calibration; payback depends on the
savings and subsequent batch count (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\paragraph{Baseline tuning and feasibility.}
IVF-PQ is evaluated at its original partition and at JHQ's selected list count.
The latter reaches 0.19--0.76 of the original throughput on five datasets;
Stella gains $1.5\times$. Each system uses its better measured partition.
CAGRA fp32 fails to build on BGE and Stella, while int8 succeeds. Successful
Stella IVF-PQ runs use reduced training and 128/256-byte codes; the reported
partition has 32,768 lists. The Stella CAGRA-int8 row marked
\texttt{CONTAMINATED} is excluded from throughput and build summaries.

\subsection{RQ2: selection quality and calibration economics}
\label{sec:rq2}
\paragraph{Variation and fixed alternatives.}
At probe 128, the six-dataset alpha sweep requires budgets from 4 to at
least 200; arxiv still improves at the largest tested value. The held-out
experiment compares fixed budgets, the sampled rule, full-pool calibration,
same-sample enumeration and an offline oracle on one index per dataset.
Even-indexed queries form a 500-query calibration pool; 500 odd-indexed queries
provide recall and timing. The oracle uses held-out truth to select the fastest
grid point within $10^{-3}$ of the maximum measured recall.

The rule's median budget matches the offline oracle in 10 of the 12 cells,
and the two it misses lie on performance plateaus. No fixed budget does as
well across the six: the oracle ranges from 4 on OpenAI-3072 to 200 on arxiv,
a fiftyfold spread, so any constant either overpays where the budget is small
or underfills where it is large.

\paragraph{Sampling risk and enumeration.}
With $S=32$, $27$--$98\%$ of draws lose more than $10^{-3}$ held-out recall;
at $S=128$ failures still range from zero to 40/64
(Figure~\ref{fig:budget}a). Four datasets reach at most 7/64 there; arxiv and
bge-m3 do not, and $S=128$ is therefore not a safe setting so much as the
largest we measured. Enumeration and bisection agree in every tested draw, at
$1.5$--$1.7\times$ the cost.

\paragraph{Gain and repayment.}
At $S=128$ the selected arm is worth $1.05$--$2.77\times$ on ten of the twelve
cells, for $5.2$--$30.8$\,ms of calibration, repaid in an estimated $1.4$--$36.4$
subsequent 500-query batches. The other two are arxiv, where the rule is
$12$--$14\%$ \emph{slower} than fixed $\alpha=100$ and so never repays: the
sufficient budget there is at or beyond $\alpha_{\max}$, and a rule that
searches a grid cannot help when the answer is outside it. That is the
condition under which this contribution does not apply, and it is visible from
the sweep before calibration is run. Payback uses the reciprocal of mean
selected fixed-arm QPS, not a new service-throughput measurement, and
calibration excludes sample gathering.

\subsection{RQ3: representation and execution ablation}
\label{sec:rq3}
\paragraph{Value of the residual hierarchy.}
Across six datasets, maximum measured primary-only recall ranges from
0.5135 to 0.8642; residual refinement extends these ranges. This ablation
retains the full allocation and measures hierarchy value rather than minimal
JQ memory.

\paragraph{Factorisation and packed access.}
With distance fixed, $G=2$ has the highest observed throughput in all eight
granularity-sweep cells (Figure~\ref{fig:lutgroups}). $G=4$ and $G=8$ each
store 16 entries per subspace, yet $G=8$ is 5.8--38.3\% slower.
Factorisation gains up to 53.8\% at $M=384$; smaller gains at $M=96$ vary
in sign across phases. Small differences do not establish general optimality
or significance.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_lutgroups.pdf}
\caption{(a) Granularity sweep, each line normalised to its $G=2$ point,
probes 8/32/128/512. (b) Separate table$\times$access phase, probes
32/128/512. Factorisation and packing are distinct changes; phases are not
pooled as repeated measurements of one configuration.}
\label{fig:lutgroups}
\end{figure}

The access experiment tests full and split tables with byte and word loads
on six datasets at four probe depths: 96 measurements and 48 paired access
ratios. Packing gains 1--66\%, with maximum four-arm recall difference 0.0001;
each cell is measured once. All arms use subspace-major layout, so the
experiment does not isolate the physical transpose. Table~\ref{tab:neg}
separately reports the cursor correction.

\subsection{RQ4: mechanisms and negative results}
\label{sec:rq4}
Table~\ref{tab:neg} shows that table footprint alone does not predict speed.
Removing redundant probe traversal changes recall by at most $10^{-4}$ across
20 pairs, with throughput effects of both signs. Table construction accounts
for 0.20--2.30\% of batch time across 18 configurations. These observations
support considering instruction work alongside memory access; they do not
isolate cache, register or occupancy effects, and static SASS counts cannot
decompose runtime into measured issue and residency costs.

\subsection{RQ5: comparison with CPU JHQ}
\label{sec:rq5}\label{sec:rq6cpu}
The CPU baseline uses the authors' artifact with build and recall fixes,
FAISS commit \texttt{4a2393741}, AVX2 and pinned threads
(\texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=close}, \texttt{OMP\_PLACES=cores}). Vogue and
OpenAI-3072 use the best recorded 16/32-thread results; arxiv and OpenAI-1536
use 32 threads. Sweeps cover $\alpha\in\{2,4,8,16,32,100\}$ and probes
128/256/512/1,024; lower probes are excluded because thread start-up dominates
short timings. Both sides use $10^{-3}$ recall buckets before interpolation.

The GPU rule curve is about 52--108 times faster over overlapping recall
coverage. Most CPU cells have one timing, so these are order-of-magnitude
comparisons. Codebooks are trained separately, and both OpenAI sets use 4,096
CPU lists versus 8,192 on the GPU frontier. CPU results for BGE and Stella
are unavailable.
