\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experi}
\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
All GPU measurements use one RTX 5090 with 32\,GB memory and 170 SMs; the
host is a Xeon Platinum 8470Q with 208 logical CPUs. The recorded baseline
software is CUDA 13.0, driver 595.71.05 and cuVS 26.08.01. Table~\ref{tab:data}
gives actual database sizes. The OpenAI, BGE and Stella conversion removes
non-finite vectors, normalises nonzero vectors, and holds queries out from the
corpus (seed 42). Stella uses corpus samples rather than its official query
split. All systems receive the same exported vectors and squared-L2 top-10
truth; no extra normalisation is applied in the search harness.

Timing covers host query input to host result output, including transfers and
synchronisation. Main frontiers answer the entire query set in one call:
1,024 is buffer capacity, not a claim of 1,024 distinct queries. JHQ rule-arm
QPS is batch size divided by mean time over three warmed calls; cuVS CSVs
report mean QPS over three runs. Frontier QPS excludes one-time calibration,
which is reported separately through break-even analysis. Repeated cold builds
on Vogue gave recalls 0.9852, 0.9842 and 0.9849; changes below roughly
$10^{-3}$ are within this observed build variation unless repeated evidence
establishes otherwise. Single-index differences retain their measured values.

JHQ uses $M=d/8$, $B=B_r=8$, 39 training points per centroid, eight coarse
iterations and 100,000 residual-training vectors. All plotted JHQ frontier
points use this denser training configuration. Its probe grid is
$\{8,32,128,256,512,1024\}$. The main frontier's calibration uses $S=32$,
$\epsilon=1$ and maximum budget 100 on the query pool itself; it is separate
from the held-out $S=128$, maximum-200 evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}.
That evaluation measures the risk of small samples rather than retroactively
validating the frontier policy.

Baselines are IVF-PQ with 8-bit subcodes and multiple code lengths, and
CAGRA in fp32 and int8. These are memory--quality trade-offs, not equal-VRAM
configurations.

IVF-PQ sweeps 96--768-byte
codes on the four smaller sets and 128--512-byte codes on the larger sets;
CAGRA sweeps graph degree 32/64, search width 1/2 and intermediate top-$k$
32--2,048 where feasible. The artifact parameter table records actual
per-dataset combinations, training fractions and failures.

Curves retain nondominated clean points. Matched-recall ratios interpolate
$\log(\mathrm{QPS})$ linearly within measured recall coverage, with no
extrapolation. Interpolated winners are observed comparisons, not significance
tests or necessarily runnable configurations. Frozen logs, source mappings and
regeneration instructions are in the repository's
\texttt{paper\_adc2026/PROTOCOL.md}.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/lrlorry/JHQ_GPU}}

\subsection{RQ1: end-to-end operating regions}
\label{sec:rq1}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} compares all main GPU baselines. JHQ is competitive
through moderate and high recall on several datasets, especially the OpenAI
embeddings. CAGRA-int8
leads JHQ in 13 of 15 comparable target cells, with $1.6$--$2.9\times$ gains
in the ten cells below recall 0.95. Thus an IVF-only comparison would miss a
strong search baseline. Construction and memory are evaluated separately.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_frontier.pdf}
\caption{Steady-state Recall@10--QPS, entire query set per call. Markers and
line styles distinguish all baselines. Endpoints are sweep endpoints. The
calibrated JHQ curve uses the frontier protocol stated in

\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why the ordering turns with dimension.}
Against CAGRA fp32 the sign of the margin tracks dimension rather than
dataset size. On the two 768-dimensional sets CAGRA fp32 is level or ahead,
by up to $1.5\times$ at matched recall; on the two OpenAI sets JHQ leads by
$1.3$--$1.8\times$. The two systems differ in both terms of the same product.
A graph walk touches hundreds of candidates where an IVF scan at these probe
depths touches tens of thousands, which favours CAGRA; JHQ reads $d/8$ bytes
a candidate in its primary stage against fp32's $4d$, which favours JHQ by
the same $32\times$ at every $d$. Since the ratio is constant, what moves with
dimension is which term dominates: at $d{=}768$ the candidate count decides,
and as $d$ grows the per-candidate cost grows with it until the narrower read
outweighs the wider walk. Stage timings measure our own half of that directly. Between vogue-768
($d{=}768$, $M{=}96$) and openai3-3072 ($d{=}3072$, $M{=}384$) the primary
scan costs $2.2$--$2.8\times$ more while the residual stage costs
$4.2$--$4.7\times$ more, at every one of six
$(\mathit{nprobe},\alpha)$ combinations measured: the scan is sub-linear in
$d$ because the factorised table and packed loading are what
Section~\ref{sec:designspace} changes, while refinement reads a byte a
dimension for each of $c_k$ survivors and is not changed by this work. The
share moves with it --- at $\alpha{=}100$, $\mathit{nprobe}{=}32$ the scan
falls from $32\%$ of the query to $22\%$ and refinement rises from $57\%$ to
$70\%$. That is the mechanism behind both contributions, since the budget
sizing $c_k$ is worth calibrating exactly when refinement dominates, and it
is equally the limit: the stage this work does not optimise is the one that
takes over at high $d$. It explains JHQ's own scaling, not CAGRA's, so the
crossing itself remains an observation. Stage timings cover two of the six
datasets.
Both OpenAI sets hold 122 vectors a list, so the comparison between them
is not confounded by partition granularity. The margin is still not monotone
in $d$ --- it is larger at 1{,}536 than at 3{,}072 --- so what the evidence
supports is a regime boundary between 768 and these two, not a rate.

\paragraph{IVF-PQ is reported at its own partition, having been offered ours.}
The archived cuVS sweep varies code size over four values and probe depth over
six and fixes \texttt{n\_lists} at one value a dataset, while JHQ's was swept
over four and the frontier reports the winner --- one side tuned on a parameter
the other was not. Re-running IVF-PQ at JHQ's value settles it in the
baseline's favour on five of the six datasets: at matched recall it reaches
$0.19$ to $0.76$ of its own archived throughput there, so the coarser
partition it already had is the better one for it. Stella-trec24 is the
exception, where the finer partition is worth $1.5\times$ at recall 0.80, and
the frontier reports it there. Otherwise the two families want opposite
granularities --- JHQ holds 122 vectors a list on openai3-1536 against
IVF-PQ's 976 --- and a cheap primary scan is what makes the fine partition
and the deep probe affordable. Each system is reported at whichever of its
measured partitions gives the better front.

The tested CAGRA-fp32 build path fails on BGE and Stella;
CAGRA-int8 succeeds on both. Stella's default IVF-PQ path fails, but a 2\%
training fraction with 16,384 lists and 128/256-byte codes succeeds. Those
search results are included, reaching recall 0.8867 in the measured grid.
They do not support comparisons above that coverage. The Stella CAGRA-int8
row flagged \texttt{CONTAMINATED} is excluded from both throughput and build
summaries. These statements concern recorded configurations on this device.

\subsection{RQ2: selection quality and calibration economics}
\label{sec:rq2}
\paragraph{Variation and fixed alternatives.}
The six-dataset alpha sweep at probe 128 spans sufficient budgets from 4 to
at least 200; the arxiv endpoint remains improving. To test whether a fixed
constant suffices, five arms share one index: fixed budgets, the sampled rule,
full-pool calibration, enumeration on the same sample, and an offline oracle.
Even-indexed queries form a 500-query calibration pool; the odd-indexed 500
queries supply held-out recall and timing. The oracle selects the fastest
measured grid point within $10^{-3}$ of the maximum measured recall, using
held-out truth only as an offline reference.

Fixed $\alpha=64$ meets the quality threshold in all four cells but gives up
about 24\% of the oracle throughput on OpenAI at probe 128. Fixed
$\alpha=8$ loses up to 0.0366 recall on Vogue. The rule's median budget
matches the oracle in the two Vogue cells; OpenAI selects 4 versus the
oracle's 8 on a nearby performance plateau. At probe 512 their recalls are
0.9800 and 0.9802: close, but not equal. Full-pool calibration chooses
100/100/4/4. Its absolute one-slot tolerance is stricter over 500 queries,
so larger samples change the criterion's relative strictness as well as its
coverage.

\paragraph{Sampling risk and enumeration.}
Figure~\ref{fig:budget}(a) reports draws whose held-out recall loss exceeds
$10^{-3}$. With $S=32$ the fraction ranges from 25\% to 69\%; with $S=128$
it ranges from zero observed failures to 5/64. Zero observations do not
establish zero risk. These are repeated draws from one fixed pool per cell,
not independent workloads. Whole-grid enumeration and bisection include the
reference search in their timers; they select the same budget in all 768
tested draws, while enumeration costs $1.5$--$1.7\times$ as much. This is
empirical agreement, not a global-minimum guarantee for the bounded selector.

\paragraph{Gain and repayment.}
At $S=128$, mean selected-arm QPS gains span $1.095$--$1.469\times$ and
calibration costs 7.8--34.8\,ms. Equation~\ref{eq:total} estimates repayment
in 4.0--19.2 subsequent 500-query batches. The larger gain repays fastest;
a small steady-state gain can require many batches. Here \texttt{mean\_qps}
is the mean of the fixed-arm throughputs selected by the draws, not a new
service-throughput measurement. The estimate uses its reciprocal; calibration
timing includes reference search and agreement checks but excludes the initial
sample gather. These are not full lifecycle latency measurements. The separate
six-dataset frontier protocol also includes a non-repaying configuration;
its economics and tolerance sweeps are archived separately rather than pooled
with this experiment.

\subsection{RQ3: representation and execution ablation}
\label{sec:rq3}
\paragraph{Value of the residual hierarchy.}
A primary-only ablation reaches maximum measured recalls between 0.5135 and
0.8642 across the six datasets. Full JHQ extends those ranges substantially;
this supports selective residual access while leaving budget selection open.
The ablation retains the full allocation and therefore measures hierarchy
value, not the memory of a minimal JQ index.

\paragraph{Factorisation and packed access.}
Figure~\ref{fig:lutgroups} varies table grouping with distance fixed. $G=2$
has the largest observed throughput in all eight granularity-sweep cells.
$G=4$ and $G=8$ both store 16 entries per subspace, yet $G=8$ is
5.8--38.3\% slower. Equal nominal table size therefore does not imply equal
speed. At $M=384$, factorisation gains up to 53.8\% in the paired
comparison; at $M=96$ gains are smaller and vary in sign across phases.
No general optimality or significance is inferred from the smallest differences.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_lutgroups.pdf}
\caption{(a) Granularity sweep, each line normalised to its $G=2$ point,
probes 8/32/128/512. (b) Separate table$\times$access phase, probes
32/128/512. Factorisation and packing are distinct changes; phases are not
pooled as repeated measurements of one configuration.}
\label{fig:lutgroups}
\end{figure}

The six-dataset layout experiment covers four probe depths and four
full/split$\times$byte/word combinations: 96 measurements and 48 paired
word-versus-byte ratios. Packing gains 1--66\%, with maximum four-arm
recall difference 0.0001. Each cell is measured once. Factorisation is the
representation-specific design and packing is representation-enabled
optimisation; both are ablated here. The cursor correction is generic but
large enough to report, and Table~\ref{tab:neg} gives it. The physical
$[N,M]\rightarrow[M,N]$ transpose is neither ablated nor claimed: it is the
common baseline of every arm in every experiment above, and restoring the
candidate-major path would mean writing a scan kernel that no longer exists
rather than setting a flag. It is standard coalescing, and we report it as
part of the design rather than as a result.

\subsection{RQ4: mechanisms and negative results}
\label{sec:rq4}
Table~\ref{tab:neg} gives counterexamples to simple design heuristics.
Table-free distance uses less table state yet runs slower; finer grouping
also adds lookups. The private probe cursor is the sharpest of them and moves
no bytes at all: it removes a redundant traversal of the probe list, changes
no distance --- recall moves by at most $10^{-4}$ across its twenty
dataset$\times$probe-depth pairs --- and is worth up to $+148.5\%$, while two
low-probe cells give back $1.4\%$. Table construction is a separate candidate
explanation and is too small to be one, at $0.20$ to $2.30\%$ of a batch over
18 configurations. These observations are consistent with instruction demand
and memory access jointly affecting performance. They do not isolate cache,
register pressure or achieved occupancy. Without hardware counters, static
SASS counts cannot assign the remaining time to residency. We consequently do
not decompose runtime into independently measured issue and residency terms.

Vogue also has an imbalanced coarse list containing about 14.5\% of its
vectors. This is an observed workload characteristic that affects routed
candidate counts, not an explanation for every performance difference.

\subsection{RQ5: what the port buys over CPU JHQ}
\label{sec:rq5}\label{sec:rq6cpu}
The CPU comparison uses the authors' JHQ artifact with documented build and
recall fixes, FAISS commit \texttt{4a2393741}, AVX2, and the best of 16/32
threads pinned with \texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=close},
\texttt{OMP\_PLACES=cores}. Its envelope sweeps
$\alpha\in\{2,4,8,16,32,100\}$ and probes 128/256/512/1,024;
lower probes are excluded because the short batch timings are dominated by
thread start-up. Both sides use $10^{-3}$ recall buckets before interpolation.
The GPU rule curve is about 52--108 times faster at the measured overlapping
recalls. Most CPU cells have one timing; these are order-of-magnitude results.
The two implementations train separate codebooks, and OpenAI-1536 uses
4,096 CPU lists versus 8,192 GPU lists. Thus this is a system comparison,
not an identical-index kernel speedup. CPU results for BGE and Stella are
unavailable; source provenance limits are explicit in the artifact.

\subsection{RQ6: construction and memory}
\label{sec:rq6}
\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_build.pdf}
\caption{Build-time medians and ranges over recorded clean configurations
and repeats, not confidence intervals. JHQ combines its cold training time
with the median and range of encoding times. Baseline bars use all recorded
clean builds; the lifecycle estimate uses their fastest builds separately.
CAGRA int8 is not drawn: only its fp32 variant appears here, and int8's
construction cost is quoted in the text where its search advantage is.}
\label{fig:build}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:build} shows a construction advantage for JHQ in the tested
configurations. JHQ's cold Stella build takes 13.4\,s against 39.0\,s for the
fastest clean IVF-PQ build, the only compared index that builds there. Where
CAGRA-int8 searches faster (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}), a model
$T_{\mathrm{build}}+Q/\mathrm{QPS}$ puts the crossover at 1.6--8.9M queries at
recall 0.90/0.95. This combines fastest baseline builds with interpolated
search frontiers and omits calibration/control overhead; it is an estimate
under those assumptions, not a measured lifecycle comparison.

Two asymmetries in that comparison, pointing opposite ways. The coarse
training budgets are not matched and cannot be: cuVS's k-means trains on half
the dataset by default, which allocates $36.4$\,GiB at Stella's
$17.8$M$\,\times\,$1024 and does not fit this card, so IVF-PQ runs there at
$2$--$10\%$ against JHQ's $7.2\%$ --- and on the four smaller sets at the
default $50\%$ against JHQ's $14$--$32\%$. Measured on bge-m3, the reduction
is worth $34\%$ of IVF-PQ's build ($32.2$\,s at the default against
$21.1$\,s reduced), so the advantage is overstated on the small sets and
understated on the large ones. Second, the advantage narrows with $N$ for a
reason internal to JHQ: training is $71\%$ of its build on vogue-768 and
$27\%$ on Stella, where encoding every vector dominates. Cheap training is
where JHQ's advantage lies, and it is the part that stops mattering as $N$
grows.

\centering

The residual representation is JHQ's largest resident component on Stella. Figure~\ref{fig:memory} includes primary/residual codes, centroids and
query workspace, with an unattributed remainder. JHQ occupies a different
memory--accuracy--throughput regime rather than having universal memory
superiority. Together, the experiments support representation-aware primary
execution and workload-specific refinement, with deployment choices depending
on quality risk, batch size and amortisation.
