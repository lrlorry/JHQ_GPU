\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experi}
\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
Measurements use one RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 170 SMs), a Xeon Platinum 8470Q
host (208 logical CPUs), CUDA 13.0, driver 595.71.05 and cuVS 26.08.01.
Table~\ref{tab:data} lists database sizes. OpenAI, BGE and Stella conversion
removes non-finite vectors, normalises nonzero vectors and holds queries out
with seed 42; Stella queries are corpus samples. All systems use the same
exported vectors and squared-L2 top-10 truth, without further normalisation.

Timing covers host query input to host result output, including transfers
and synchronisation. Frontiers process all $n_q$ queries in one call using
1,024-capacity buffers. JHQ reports batch size divided by mean time over three
warmed calls; cuVS reports mean QPS over three runs. Frontier QPS excludes
one-time calibration, accounted for separately through break-even analysis.
Vogue cold-build recalls of 0.9852, 0.9842 and 0.9849 establish an observed
variation of roughly $10^{-3}$; smaller changes require repeated evidence for
interpretation. Single-index differences retain their measured values.

JHQ uses $M=d/8$, $B=B_r=8$, 39 training points per centroid, eight coarse
iterations and 100,000 residual-training vectors. All JHQ frontier points use
this training setup and probes $\{8,32,128,256,512,1024\}$. Frontier
calibration uses $S=32$, $\epsilon=1$ and $\alpha_{\max}=100$ on the query
pool itself. The separate held-out evaluation uses $S=128$ and
$\alpha_{\max}=200$ (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}); its quality results do not
validate the frontier's calibration protocol.

IVF-PQ sweeps 8-bit subcodes with 96--768-byte codes on the four smaller sets
and 128--512-byte codes on the larger sets. CAGRA sweeps graph
degree 32/64, search width 1/2 and intermediate top-$k$ 32--2,048 where
feasible. These configurations offer different memory and quality trade-offs;
GPU memory is not matched.

Curves retain nondominated clean points. Matched-recall ratios linearly
interpolate $\log(\mathrm{QPS})$ within measured coverage, without
extrapolation; they are descriptive comparisons, not significance tests or
necessarily runnable configurations. The implementation and plotting scripts
are available in the project repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/lrlorry/JHQ_GPU}}

\subsection{RQ1: end-to-end operating regions}
\label{sec:rq1}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} compares JHQ with IVF-PQ and CAGRA fp32. CAGRA
fp32 leads on the two 768-dimensional sets and JHQ on the two OpenAI ones;
IVF-PQ is behind throughout.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_frontier.pdf}
\caption{Steady-state Recall@10--QPS, entire query set per call. Markers and
line styles distinguish all baselines. Endpoints are sweep endpoints. The
calibrated JHQ curve uses the frontier protocol stated in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}, not the held-out budget experiment.}
\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Operating regions and stage costs.}
At matched recall, CAGRA fp32 is level or up to $1.5\times$ faster on the
768-dimensional datasets; JHQ is $1.3$--$1.8\times$ faster on the OpenAI
datasets. These cross-dataset observations do not isolate a dimension effect.
Separate JHQ stage measurements show refinement taking 57\% of timed work on
Vogue and 70\% on OpenAI-3072 at $\alpha=100$, $\mathit{nprobe}=32$.
The refinement share motivates budget calibration; payback depends on the
savings and subsequent batch count (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\paragraph{Baseline tuning and feasibility.}
IVF-PQ is evaluated at its original partition and at JHQ's selected list count.
The latter reaches 0.19--0.76 of the original throughput on five datasets;
Stella gains $1.5\times$. Each system uses its better measured partition.
CAGRA fails to build on BGE and Stella. Successful
Stella IVF-PQ runs use reduced training and 128/256-byte codes; the reported
partition has 32,768 lists. One Stella row marked \texttt{CONTAMINATED} in
the harness's own record is excluded from throughput and build summaries.

\subsection{RQ2: selection quality and calibration economics}
\label{sec:rq2}
\paragraph{Variation and fixed alternatives.}
At probe 128, the six-dataset alpha sweep requires budgets from 4 to at
least 200; arxiv still improves at the largest tested value. The held-out
experiment compares fixed budgets, the sampled rule, full-pool calibration,
same-sample enumeration and an offline oracle on one index per dataset.
Even-indexed queries form a 500-query calibration pool. Odd-indexed queries
provide recall and timing: 499 on Stella and 500 on the other datasets. The oracle uses held-out truth to select the fastest
grid point within $10^{-3}$ of the maximum measured recall.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} gives the five arms per cell. The rule's median budget
matches the oracle in 10 of 12 cells. The exceptions
are arxiv at probe 128 and OpenAI-3072 at probe 512. Oracle budgets range from
4 to 200, exposing the trade-off of a shared constant. Median budget agreement
does not establish held-out quality for every draw.

\paragraph{Sampling risk and enumeration.}
With $S=32$, 27--98\% of draws lose more than $10^{-3}$ held-out recall;
at $S=128$, failures range from zero to 40/64 (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}a).
These are repeated draws from fixed pools; zero observations do not establish
zero risk. Enumeration and bisection agree in all 2,304 draws across
$S=32,64,128$. Enumeration costs $1.66$--$2.00\times$ as much, with reference
search included in both timers.

\paragraph{Gain and repayment.}
At $S=128$, selected-arm QPS gains are $1.05$--$2.77\times$ in ten of twelve
cells, with 5.2--30.8\,ms calibration and estimated payback of 1.4--36.4
500-query batches. The two arxiv cells have 12--14\% lower QPS than fixed
$\alpha=100$, so calibration does not repay. Payback uses the reciprocal of
mean selected fixed-arm QPS, not a measured service workload. Calibration
includes reference search and agreement checks but excludes sample gathering.

\subsection{RQ3: what the representation is worth, and what it is not}
\label{sec:rq3}\label{sec:rq4}
Every experiment here holds the represented distance fixed and varies one
choice, so Table~\ref{tab:neg} collects them with the heuristic each one
refutes; \emph{cells} is how many paired comparisons the range spans.

\paragraph{The hierarchy.} Primary-only recall tops out at 0.5135--0.8642
across the six datasets, and residual refinement extends every range. The
ablation keeps the full allocation, so it measures what the second level buys
rather than what a minimal JQ index would cost.

\paragraph{Granularity.} With distance fixed, $G=2$ has the highest observed
throughput in all eight granularity-sweep cells (Figure~\ref{fig:lutgroups}).
The sharpest form of the argument is between the losers: $G=4$ and $G=8$ store
16 entries a subspace each, so footprint is constant and only lookups differ,
and $G=8$ is 5.8--38.3\% slower. Factorisation gains up to 53.8\% at $M=384$;
at $M=96$ the gains are small and vary in sign, which is the regime where the
full table nearly fits.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_lutgroups.pdf}
\caption{(a) Granularity sweep, each line normalised to its $G=2$ point,
probes 8/32/128/512. (b) Separate table$\times$access phase, probes
32/128/512. Phases are not pooled as repeated measurements of one
configuration.}
\label{fig:lutgroups}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Access.} Full and split tables with byte and word loads, six
datasets at four probe depths: 96 measurements, 48 paired ratios, packing
worth 1--66\% with a maximum four-arm recall difference of 0.0001. Every arm
uses the subspace-major layout, so this does not isolate the physical
transpose, which is standard coalescing and is not claimed.

\paragraph{What none of it isolates.} Table footprint alone does not predict
speed: removing a redundant probe traversal moves no bytes, changes recall by
at most $10^{-4}$ over 20 pairs, and changes throughput in both directions.
Table construction is too small to be the explanation instead, at
0.20--2.30\% of batch time over 18 configurations. Instruction work and memory
access evidently both matter, but without hardware counters we cannot separate
cache, register pressure and occupancy, and static SASS counts cannot
decompose runtime into issue and residency terms. Vogue's largest coarse list
also holds about 14.5\% of its vectors, an imbalance that affects routed
candidate counts.

\subsection{RQ4: comparison with CPU JHQ}
\label{sec:rq5}\label{sec:rq6cpu}
The CPU baseline uses the authors' artifact with build and recall fixes,
FAISS commit \texttt{4a2393741} and AVX2. Threads are pinned with
\texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=close} and \texttt{OMP\_PLACES=cores}. Vogue and
OpenAI-3072 use the best recorded 16/32-thread results; arxiv and OpenAI-1536
use 32 threads. The sweep tests budgets 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 100, with
probes 128, 256, 512 and 1,024. Lower probes are excluded because thread
start-up dominates short timings. Both sides use $10^{-3}$ recall buckets before interpolation.

The GPU rule curve is about 52--108 times faster over overlapping recall
coverage. Most CPU cells have one timing, so these are order-of-magnitude
comparisons. Codebooks are trained separately, and both OpenAI sets use 4,096
CPU lists versus 8,192 on the GPU frontier. CPU results for BGE and Stella
are unavailable.
