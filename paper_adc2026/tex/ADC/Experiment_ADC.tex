\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experi}
\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
Measurements use one RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 170 SMs), a Xeon Platinum 8470Q
host (208 logical CPUs), CUDA 13.0, driver 595.71.05 and cuVS 26.08.01.
Table~\ref{tab:data} cites the source datasets and lists database sizes.
The OpenAI sets contain DBpedia embeddings; BGE uses Italian Wikipedia,
and Stella uses the TREC24 BioGen corpus. OpenAI, BGE and Stella conversion
removes non-finite vectors, normalises nonzero vectors and holds queries out
with seed 42; Stella queries are corpus samples. All systems use the same
exported vectors and squared-L2 top-10 truth, without further normalisation.

Timing covers host query input to host result output. Frontiers answer all
$n_q$ queries in one call and report the mean of three warmed runs; frontier
QPS excludes calibration. Section~\ref{sec:rq2} measures calibration cost
under its separate held-out protocol. Three cold builds of Vogue recall
0.9852, 0.9842 and 0.9849, so
differences below about $10^{-3}$ across builds need repeated evidence;
differences within one index do not.

JHQ uses $M=d/8$, $B=B_r=8$, 39 training points per centroid, eight coarse
iterations and 100,000 residual-training vectors. All JHQ frontier points use
this training setup and probes $\{8,32,128,256,512,1024\}$. The primary
system comparison fixes $\alpha=100$. The additional same-pool calibration
curve uses $S=32$, $\epsilon=1$ and $\alpha_{\max}=100$ on the evaluated
query pool. The separate held-out evaluation uses $S=128$ and
$\alpha_{\max}=200$ (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}); its quality results do not
validate the frontier's calibration protocol.

IVF-PQ sweeps 8-bit subcodes at 96--768-byte code lengths and CAGRA sweeps
graph degree, search width and intermediate top-$k$; GPU memory is not
matched. Curves keep nondominated clean points and matched-recall ratios
interpolate $\log(\mathrm{QPS})$ without extrapolating. The implementation,
harness and plotting scripts will be released on publication.
GPU IVF-RaBitQ is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related} but not measured
against. The cuVS implementation exposes probe count and a search kernel and
no re-ranking stage, and at one bit per dimension --- the configuration the
method is known for --- Recall@10 does not exceed $0.85$ here at any probe
depth. Reaching the recalls compared in this section needs eight bits and a
scan eight times more expensive, at which point it measures slower than
CAGRA-int8 at $R{=}0.90$--$0.95$, inverting its own published ordering. That
is a statement about the configuration available to us, so nothing here
establishes superiority over the method.

\subsection{RQ1: end-to-end operating regions}
\label{sec:rq1}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} compares fixed-budget JHQ with IVF-PQ and CAGRA
fp32. CAGRA leads on the two 768-dimensional sets at recall 0.90/0.95;
the ordering on the OpenAI sets depends on the target recall.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_frontier.pdf}
\caption{Recall@10--QPS, entire query set per call. Solid JHQ fixes
$\alpha=100$. Dashed JHQ calibrates on the evaluated query pool and excludes
calibration cost; it does not validate reuse on unseen queries.
Section~\ref{sec:rq2} evaluates calibration held out. Endpoints are measured
sweep endpoints.}
\label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Operating regions and stage costs.}
At recall 0.90/0.95, CAGRA fp32 is $1.45$--$2.03\times$ faster than fixed
JHQ on the 768-dimensional datasets. On OpenAI-1536, JHQ is near parity at
0.95 and $1.52\times$ faster at 0.98; on OpenAI-3072 its QPS is $0.66$ and
$1.09$ times CAGRA's at these recalls. IVF-PQ is slower at the evaluated
targets within common measured coverage. These cross-dataset observations
do not isolate a dimension effect.
Separate JHQ stage measurements show refinement taking 57\% of timed work on
Vogue and 70\% on OpenAI-3072 at $\alpha=100$, $\mathit{nprobe}=32$.
The refinement share motivates budget calibration; payback depends on the
savings and subsequent batch count (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\paragraph{Baseline tuning and feasibility.}
IVF-PQ was run at its own partition and at JHQ's; at JHQ's it reaches only
$0.19$--$0.76$ of its own throughput on five datasets and gains $1.5\times$ on
Stella, so each system is reported at its better measured partition. CAGRA
fails to build on BGE and Stella, and one Stella row marked
\texttt{CONTAMINATED} by the harness is excluded throughout.

\subsection{RQ2: selection quality and calibration economics}
\label{sec:rq2}
\paragraph{Variation and fixed alternatives.}
At probe 128 the six-dataset sweep needs budgets from 4 to at least 200, with
arxiv still improving at the largest tested value. Five arms share one index
per dataset --- fixed budgets, the sampled rule, full-pool calibration,
same-sample enumeration, and an offline oracle that uses held-out truth to
pick the fastest grid point within $10^{-3}$ of the best measured recall.
Even-indexed queries calibrate, odd-indexed ones measure.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} gives the five arms per cell. The rule's median budget
matches the oracle in 10 of 12 cells. The exceptions
are arxiv at probe 128 and OpenAI-3072 at probe 512. Oracle budgets range from
4 to 200, exposing the trade-off of a shared constant. Median budget agreement
does not establish held-out quality for every draw.

\paragraph{Sampling risk and enumeration.}
With $S=32$, 27--98\% of draws lose more than $10^{-3}$ held-out recall;
at $S=128$, failures range from zero to 40/64 (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}a).
These are repeated draws from fixed pools; zero observations do not establish
zero risk. With fixed $\epsilon=1$, increasing $S$ also tightens the allowed
disagreement fraction, so this is not an isolated sample-size effect.
Enumeration and bisection agree in all 2,304 draws across
$S=32,64,128$. Enumeration costs $1.66$--$2.00\times$ as much, with reference
search included in both timers.


\paragraph{Gain and repayment.}
At $S=128$, mean selected-arm QPS gains are $1.05$--$2.77\times$ in ten of twelve
cells, with 5.2--30.8\,ms calibration and estimated payback of 1.4--36.4
500-query batches. Gains do not imply reliable satisfaction of the quality
threshold: BGE at probe 512 gains $1.05\times$ but exceeds it in 24/64 draws.
On arxiv, QPS is 12--14\% lower than fixed $\alpha=100$, while mean recall
increases from 0.9748/0.9878 to 0.9751/0.9885 at probes 128/512; larger
budgets trade speed for quality. Calibration does not repay in throughput
there. Payback uses the reciprocal of mean selected fixed-arm QPS, which
underestimates mean batch time when selections differ and gives an optimistic
aggregate estimate. Calibration includes reference search and agreement
checks but excludes sample gathering.

\subsection{RQ3: what the representation is worth, and what it is not}
\label{sec:rq3}\label{sec:rq4}
Every experiment here holds the represented distance fixed and varies one
choice, so Table~\ref{tab:neg} collects them with the heuristic each one
refutes; \emph{cells} is how many paired comparisons the range spans.

\begin{table}[tbp]
\centering
\caption{Paired observations, each holding the represented distance fixed.
\emph{Cells} counts comparisons within a source experiment; the ranges do not
describe a common workload.}
\label{tab:neg}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{ll@{\hspace{1.2em}}r@{\hspace{1.2em}}l}\toprule
Change & QPS effect & cells & Simple heuristic challenged\\\midrule
Table-free exact score & $-30$ to $-52\%$ & 12 & Minimum table state\\
$G=8$ versus $G=4$ & $-3.4$ to $-38.5\%$ & 24 & Equal footprint, equal speed\\
Packed 32-bit loads & $+1$ to $+66\%$ & 48 & Bytes moved predict speed\\
Private probe cursor & $-1.4$ to $+148.5\%$ & 20 & Loop work is negligible\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{The hierarchy.} Primary-only recall tops out at 0.5135--0.8642
across the six datasets, and residual refinement extends every range. The
ablation keeps the full allocation, so it measures what the second level buys
rather than what a minimal JQ index would cost.

\paragraph{Granularity.} With distance fixed, $G=2$ has the highest observed
throughput in 23 of the 24 cells spanning six datasets and four probe depths
(Figure~\ref{fig:lutgroups}); the exception is arxiv-768 at probe 128, where
$G=1$ leads by $0.03\%$. The sharpest form of the argument is between the
losers: $G=4$ and $G=8$ store 16 entries a subspace each, so footprint is
constant and only lookups differ, and $G=8$ is 3.4--38.5\% slower over all 24
pairs, widening with probe depth. Factorisation grows with $M$: $-0.0$ to
$+9.9\%$ at $M{=}96$, where the full table nearly fits, and up to $+53.4\%$
at $M{=}192$.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figs/fig_lutgroups.pdf}
\caption{Throughput against the full table, six datasets at four probe
depths; lines are medians over datasets and bands their range. Factorising to
$G{=}2$ gains, factorising further gives it back, and both effects grow with
probe depth. $4{\times}4$ and $8{\times}2$ store the same 16 entries and
differ only in lookups.}
\label{fig:lutgroups}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Access.} Full and split tables with byte and word loads, six
datasets at four probe depths: 96 measurements, 48 paired ratios, packing
worth 1--66\% with a maximum four-arm recall difference of 0.0001.
Factorisation pays under both layouts and pays more with depth, so the two
changes largely multiply rather than substitute. Every arm
uses the subspace-major layout, so this does not isolate the physical
transpose, which is standard coalescing and is not claimed.


\paragraph{What none of it isolates.} Footprint alone does not predict speed
(Table~\ref{tab:neg}):
removing a redundant probe traversal moves no bytes, changes recall by at most
$10^{-4}$ over 20 pairs, and moves throughput in both directions. Table
construction cannot be the explanation instead, at 0.20--2.30\% of batch time.
Instruction work and memory access both matter, but without hardware counters
we cannot separate cache, register pressure and occupancy. Vogue's largest
coarse list also holds about 14.5\% of its vectors, which affects routed
candidate counts.

\subsection{RQ4: comparison with CPU JHQ}
\label{sec:rq5}\label{sec:rq6cpu}
Against the authors' own CPU artifact, the GPU same-pool rule curve is about 52--108
times faster over the recalls both cover. The more useful number is what the
budget is worth on each processor: the same $\alpha$ knob buys $2.5$ to
$3.0\times$ more on the GPU than on the CPU at every probe depth on
openai3-3072. On the CPU the scan dominates and the budget barely moves
throughput; on the GPU the scan is what Section~\ref{sec:designspace} makes
cheap, so refinement takes the larger share and the same cut in survivors
buys more. The observations motivate retuning the inherited refinement
budget for the GPU execution path.

It is a system comparison, not an identical-index speedup. The two
implementations train separate codebooks, both OpenAI sets use 4{,}096 CPU
lists against 8{,}192 on the GPU frontier, probes below 128 are excluded
because thread start-up dominates their timings, and most CPU cells are timed
once, so these are order-of-magnitude figures. BGE and Stella have no CPU
measurement.

\subsection{RQ5: construction and memory}
\label{sec:rq6}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_build.pdf}
\caption{Index build time, log scale. Bars are medians over clean

JHQ's bar is one cold training plus its median encode.}

Among the compared systems and configurations, JHQ has the lowest build
estimate on all six datasets, by $3.5$--$8.5\times$ over the fastest compared
alternative (Figure~\ref{fig:build}). Faster construction can favour workloads
with frequent rebuilds, while a faster search path can repay its larger build
cost over sufficiently many queries.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{figs/fig_memory.pdf}
\caption{Resident memory by component. Markers are measured totals; the
hatched band is the remainder we cannot attribute.}
\label{fig:memory}
\end{figure}

Training budgets differ: Stella IVF-PQ uses a reduced sample because default
training exceeds memory. This limits attribution of build differences to the
quantisers alone. Within JHQ, training accounts for 71\% of the plotted build
estimate on Vogue and 27\% on Stella, where encoding dominates.

That advantage does not extend to memory (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). The
residual level dominates every index and grows with $N$ --- $48\%$ of
$1.4$\,GiB on Vogue, $83\%$ of $20.4$\,GiB on Stella --- and it is the level that buys the high-recall end
of Section~\ref{sec:rq1}, so JHQ occupies a different
memory--accuracy--throughput point rather than a better one. An unattributed
remainder of $4$--$41\%$ is plotted rather than assigned a cause we did not
measure.
