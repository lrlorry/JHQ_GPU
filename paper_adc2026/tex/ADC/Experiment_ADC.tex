\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:experi}

\subsection{Setup and protocol}
\label{sec:setup}
All GPU systems run on one NVIDIA RTX 5090: 32{,}607 MiB device memory, 170 SMs,
a 101{,}376-byte opt-in shared-memory limit per block; the host has 208 logical
cores and 754 GB RAM. Datasets are vogue-768 ($1.0\text{M}\times 768$),
arxiv-768 ($2.25\text{M}\times 768$), bge-m3 ($10.1\text{M}\times 1024$),
stella-trec24 ($17.8\text{M}\times 1024$), openai3-1536
($1.0\text{M}\times 1536$) and openai3-3072 ($1.0\text{M}\times 3072$).

We report Recall@10 against true top-10 ground truth at $k{=}10$; because $c_k$
scales with $\alpha k$, other $k$ are not established. Timing runs host query
input to host result output, and frontiers use steady-state throughput at batch
1{,}024 --- the whole query set in one call --- which Section~\ref{sec:rq5}
shows is a consequential choice. Occupancy statements are analytical
resource-budget calculations: Nsight Compute counters were unavailable in the
container (\texttt{ERR\_NVGPUCTRPERM}), so we report none measured. Two recall
noise floors are kept distinct: about $10^{-3}$ across builds in the documented
cold-start experiment, and $\approx 10^{-4}$ for paired comparisons on one index
from top-$c_k$ tie breaking. Fronts bucket recall at $10^{-3}$ so a
tie-break-sized difference cannot buy a point a place, and every quoted ratio is
the median of its one to three repeats. We do not enlarge batches by duplicating
the roughly 1k available queries: duplication alone raises throughput
$4$--$26\%$, so such a batch would measure an artefact.

Baselines are the cuVS implementations of IVF-RaBitQ~\cite{ivfrabitq}, IVF-PQ,
and CAGRA~\cite{cagra} in fp32 and int8. Our primary comparison line is the
quantised IVF family, which shares JHQ's routing structure and interface; CAGRA
is a graph index with a different build-cost profile and we report it as
context, in both variants, without excluding the one that wins. JHQ runs use
dense coarse training, $\texttt{JHQ\_N\_TRAIN}=39\cdot\mathit{nlist}$; we mix in
no under-trained points and union no fronts across binaries. The CPU JHQ
baseline runs the same index parameters on the same host with threads pinned
(\texttt{OMP\_PROC\_BIND=close}, \texttt{OMP\_PLACES=cores}) and its own
$(\alpha,\mathit{nprobe})$ sweep, sharing the training procedure but not a
codebook, with one measurement per cell.

\subsection{RQ1: where does JHQ actually lead?}
\label{sec:rq1}
Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} gives the measured frontiers and Table~\ref{tab:front}
reduces them to a single question: at each matched recall, which system is
fastest? JHQ owns 4 of the 26 comparable cells, and all four are at
Recall@$10\ge 0.97$. Below $0.95$ CAGRA-int8 leads on every dataset where both
run, by $1.6$--$2.9\times$. We state this rather than scope it away, because it
bounds the claim precisely: JHQ's region is the high-recall end, most clearly at
high dimension, where openai3-1536 leads at $0.97$ and $0.98$ and openai3-3072
at $0.97$.

  cuVS IVF-RaBitQ; ``--'' is a recall no two systems both reach. JHQ is fastest

Two things beside the table complete the picture. On bge-m3 and stella-trec24
the tested cuVS IVF-RaBitQ host-input build path fails with an out-of-memory
allocation after entering streaming construction and CAGRA fp32 does not fit the
card, while JHQ indexes both --- a statement about the measured library and
configuration paths on this device, not about those algorithms in general. And
the throughput ordering has a build-time counterpart: JHQ builds in
$1.1$--$14.3$\,s against $9$--$75$\,s for CAGRA int8. JHQ therefore trades
search throughput below $0.95$ for build time and for indexability at a code
budget --- about 9 bits per dimension --- that is not smaller than int8's.

\subsection{RQ2: does the design prediction hold?}
\label{sec:rq3}
Section~\ref{sec:designspace} predicted that reducing table state helps only
until extra lookups outweigh the residency saved. Figure~\ref{fig:lutgroups}
varies $G$ with the represented distance fixed. If state alone governed
throughput, $G{=}4$ and $G{=}8$ should win, since they store fewer entries than
$G{=}2$. They do not: $G{=}2$ is never beaten across the eight configurations,
with one exact tie against $G{=}1$, and throughput falls as lookups per
candidate rise. The sharpest form is internal to the losers. $G\cdot 2^{8/G}$ is
16 for both $G{=}4$ and $G{=}8$, so they occupy the same nominal table footprint
and differ in the number of lookups and the address arithmetic those lookups
need; the eight-way form is $5.8\%$ slower at $\mathit{nprobe}{=}8$ and $38.3\%$
slower at $512$. Residency is held fixed and issued work rises --- which is why
this pair, rather than the table-free control, separates the two effects most
cleanly.

The regime change with $M$ appears as predicted. Against the full table,
factorisation is worth $0$--$11\%$ at $M{=}96$ and $8$--$53\%$ at $M{=}384$, and
the gain rises monotonically with probe depth in both: on openai3-3072
$+8.3\%$, $+9.8\%$, $+22.6\%$ and $+53.4\%$ at $\mathit{nprobe}=8, 32, 128, 512$.
We report the trend rather than a flat interval because the trend is the
mechanism --- a table that misses is paid once per candidate per subspace, so
its cost grows with $M$ and with the candidates scanned together. The failure
edge is kept too: at $M{=}96$, $\mathit{nprobe}{=}32$ on the packed layout,
factorisation loses $4.2\%$ against the full table in the slowest repeat.

  \caption{(a) $G{=}2$ is never beaten although $G{=}4$ and $G{=}8$ store fewer
  entries and occupy the same footprint as each other. (b) Factorisation matters
  far more at $M{=}384$ than $M{=}96$ and stays complementary to packed loading;
  panel (b) is the two-by-two layout phase at

\subsection{RQ3: can the byte-count explanation be rejected?}
\label{sec:rq4}
Table~\ref{tab:neg} collects the controls that vary bytes and issued work
independently. Removing almost all table state loses $30$--$52\%$ even though it
raises the resident-block count the shared-memory budget implies --- so neither
table residency nor attainable occupancy alone is the bottleneck. Moving
identical bytes in 32-bit words gains $30$--$48\%$. Giving each thread a private
probe cursor removes redundant inner-loop work for up to $+148\%$; it is an
implementation correction, not a contribution, and its value here is diagnostic.
Fusing residual refinement removes an intermediate round trip and still loses,
because the fused kernel carries both phases' shared-memory and register
footprints for the whole scan. LUT construction is $0.2$--$2.3\%$ of a batch,
which rules out faster table building as the explanation for factorisation.

\begin{table}[tbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Controls that separate bytes moved from work issued. Every row holds
  the represented distance fixed.}
  \label{tab:neg}
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    change & effect on throughput & what it rules out \\
    \midrule
    table-free exact distance & $-30$ to $-52\%$ & least state is fastest \\
    $G{=}8$ against $G{=}4$   & $-5.8$ to $-38.3\%$ & same footprint, same speed \\
    packed 32-bit loads       & $+30$ to $+48\%$ & bytes moved predict speed \\
    private probe cursor      & $+2.5$ to $+148\%$ & loop work is negligible \\
    fused residual refinement & $-14.2\%$, $-14.8\%$ & fewer kernels is better \\
    query duplication $D{=}8$ & $+4$ to $+26\%$ & reuse alone drives the scan \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

These controls rule out competing explanations but do not measure the variable
they implicate. Static instruction counts do not need a profiler, so we
disassemble the emitted kernel. \texttt{cuobjdump -sass} on
\texttt{scan\_ivf\_coalesced\_kernel} gives 42, 84 and 168 generic loads at
$G{=}1$, $2$ and $4$ --- one, two and four lookups across 42 statically
unrolled subspace iterations --- and 144 at $G{=}8$, which is eight lookups over
18 iterations, the compiler having unrolled less because the body grew.
Floating-point adds per iteration are $G{+}1$ at every $G$, an independent check
that the normalisation is right. Counting the lookup and the work that feeds it,
issued instructions per candidate--sub\-space are $4.4$, $13.0$, $25.0$ and $50.4$.

That is an axis the controls did not have, and it separates the two costs
Section~\ref{sec:designspace} trades between (Figure~\ref{fig:issue}). Where the
table is resident --- $G{=}2$, $4$, $8$ --- query time is close to linear in
issued work, and the slope is the issue cost. $G{=}1$ issues the least work of
all four and is nonetheless slower than the line predicts, by $8$, $4$, $33$ and
$75\%$ on vogue-768 and $11$, $16$, $44$ and $120\%$ on openai3-3072 at
$\mathit{nprobe}=8$, $32$, $128$ and $512$. That gap is the residency cost, it
is the one thing the instruction line cannot explain, and it grows with probe
depth and with $M$ exactly as ``a table that misses is paid once per candidate
per subspace'' predicts. $G{=}2$ wins because it is the only granularity that
pays little of either cost. We still measure one architecture, so cross-GPU
validation stays open, but the mechanism itself is no longer an inference from a
family of controls.

  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig_issue.pdf}
  \caption{The two costs, measured separately. (a) With the table resident
  ($G{=}2,4,8$) query time is close to linear in issued instructions per
  candidate--sub\-space, counted from the disassembled kernel; $G{=}1$ issues the
  fewest and still sits above that line, and the gap is the residency cost.
  (b) That gap grows with probe depth and is larger at $M{=}384$ than $M{=}96$.}

\subsection{RQ4: what does the budget rule cost and return?}
\label{sec:rq2}
Three things must hold together for the policy to be usable. \emph{Gain}: across
32 configurations where the rule changes the budget, steady-state throughput
improves $1.044$--$2.653\times$. \emph{Quality cost}: the rule gives up at most
$0.0048$ recall over 36 configurations, so the gain is not free.
\emph{Amortisation}: payback ranges $0.5$--$21.0$ batches with median $1.75$;
large payback near unit gain is partly definitional through the denominator, so
we do not present that correlation as a mechanism.

The fourth thing is sampling risk, and it is where the policy is weakest. In
2{,}000 held-out resampled draws, $S{=}32$ keeps held-out loss within $10^{-3}$
in $76\%$ of draws for openai3-3072 but only $27\%$ for vogue-768, whose mean
loss at $S{=}32$ is $0.0033$ with a 95th percentile of $0.0110$. Openai3-3072
reaches $98\%$ at $S{=}64$; vogue still has $11\%$ of draws outside the
threshold at $S{=}128$. $S{=}32$ is therefore not a universal knee, and a
deployment must pick $S$ against a stated risk target.
Tolerance behaves similarly --- zero disagreeing slots leaves about $26\%$ of
attainable throughput unclaimed, two costs about $0.0035$ recall --- and at
$\mathit{nprobe}{=}128$ the rule recovers the swept $\alpha$ in three of four
configurations, the fourth censored by $\alpha_{\max}$ rather than solved. The
$\mathit{nprobe}{=}512$ full sweep is unavailable and is not inferred.

\subsection{RQ5: batch size and the CPU reference}
\label{sec:rq5}
Table~\ref{tab:batch} gives matched-recall ratios against IVF-RaBitQ at four
batch sizes. Two readings matter: on openai3-3072 JHQ leads at every measured
point, and on vogue-768 it crosses parity between batch 128 and 512. Batch size
can therefore change the ordering, and the frontier ratios of
Section~\ref{sec:rq1} should be read as the batch-1{,}024 column of this sweep
rather than as batch-independent summaries. The openai3-3072 batch-1{,}024,
$R{=}0.97$ cell is excluded: its RaBitQ anchor is the one matched point that
failed the project's monotonicity check.

The CPU reference answers a different question: what the port buys over CPU JHQ
at the same index parameters. Sweeping $\alpha$ and $\mathit{nprobe}$ on both
sides, the GPU reaches $52$--$54\times$ the CPU envelope on vogue-768 across
Recall@$10$ of $0.9645$ to $0.9905$, and $75$--$77\times$ on openai3-3072 from
$0.9414$ to $0.9813$; pinning $\alpha{=}100$ on both sides gives $43$--$48\times$
and $35$--$54\times$. Above Recall@10 of $0.9911$ and $0.9900$ respectively the
CPU sweep has no points, and those GPU operating points are reported as outside
its range rather than divided by the CPU's last measurement. Three limits apply.
The GPU side of the vogue comparison is the rule's chosen $\alpha$ at each
$\mathit{nprobe}$, not an exhaustive $\alpha$ sweep. CPU rows below
$\mathit{nprobe}{=}128$ are dropped, because 1{,}000 queries complete fast enough
there that thread start-up dominates the timer and measured throughput rises
with $\mathit{nprobe}$; the recall column is unaffected. And each cell is
measured once, so the ratios are order-of-magnitude figures --- the envelope
keeps the fastest point per recall bucket, which biases the CPU side upward and
so does not inflate the ratio, but with one measurement per cell we do not claim
a statistical bound.

\subsection{Construction and memory}
\label{sec:rq6}
Build times are $1.1$--$14.3$\,s for JHQ, $9$--$75$\,s for CAGRA int8,
$6.4$--$43$\,s for IVF-PQ and $4.5$--$14.0$\,s for IVF-RaBitQ where that path
builds --- tested configurations, not intrinsic bounds. Accounting must respect
caching: training can be cached while add is not, so taking maxima independently
and summing them fabricates a time that never occurred. On memory we claim no
advantage: in the four datasets with measured VRAM IVF-RaBitQ uses $22$--$39\%$
less than JHQ, and JHQ's budget is not smaller than int8's. JHQ occupies a
different memory--accuracy--throughput point and still indexes the two largest
datasets where the compared paths fail. The accounting carries an unattributed
component that should not be relabelled as allocator overhead without direct
measurement.
